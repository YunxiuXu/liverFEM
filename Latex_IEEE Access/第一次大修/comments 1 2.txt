Reviewers' Comments to Author:

Reviewer: 1

Comments:
The paper presents an interesting real-time haptic simulation prototype for liver lesion localization. The integration of GB-cFEM, hand tracking, a fingertip haptic device, and a lesion-search task is potentially useful, and the technical validation provides encouraging evidence that the system can operate on consumer-grade hardware. The manuscript is also relatively transparent about several limitations, including simplified anatomy, lack of nonlinear tissue behavior, and the limited dimensionality of the haptic feedback. However, the current version should be revised before publication.

(1) The contribution should be framed more clearly as a system-integration and technical-feasibility study, rather than as evidence of training effectiveness. The formative evaluation with one surgical expert is useful for identifying engineering limitations, but it cannot support strong claims about clinical-grade training, educational impact, or learning efficiency. Please revise the abstract, discussion, and conclusion to avoid overstatement.

(2) The haptic rendering pipeline needs more technical detail. Please report the force range of the device, force calibration procedure, possible saturation, end-to-end latency, filtering or smoothing, and the way force commands are synchronized between the physics update rate and the 1000 Hz haptic loop. If possible, include measured force-output data rather than only the computed normal-force mapping.

(3) The formative evaluation design also requires clarification. The haptics-off and haptics-on trials used different lesion positions, and the order appears fixed. This introduces possible differences in lesion difficulty and learning/familiarization effects. Please explain how lesion positions were selected and whether they were comparable in size, depth, and difficulty. The paper would be stronger if it included quantitative task metrics such as localization accuracy, time to localization, number of exploratory presses, trajectory, confidence, or error distance.

(4) The comparison with XPBD and VegaFEM is useful, but more implementation details are needed to support reproducibility and fairness. Please specify substeps, iterations, timestep, material parameters, loading conditions, mesh properties, and stopping criteria. Since the mesh scales are not always matched, the conclusions should be phrased cautiously.

(5) Finally, please perform a careful formatting and proofreading pass. Some spacing and typesetting artifacts appear in the manuscript, such as “ABSTRACTHaptic”, “INDEX TERMSLiver”, “V egaFEM”, “Y oung’s”, “RESUL TS”, “Chernikovaet al.”, and “Agbohet al.” These may be conversion artifacts in my version, but they should be corrected in the final submission.

1) Does the paper contribute to the body of knowledge?: The paper makes a useful contribution if it is framed as a system-integration and technical-feasibility study. Its main value is not the proposal of a new FEM algorithm or a new haptic device, since both components are largely based on prior work by the authors, but the integration of a GB-cFEM soft-tissue simulation core, hand tracking, 1-DOF fingertip haptic feedback, and a liver lesion localization task into a working real-time prototype. This can contribute to the body of knowledge in medical haptic simulation by documenting practical engineering constraints and by identifying limitations through formative expert feedback. However, the contribution should be stated more cautiously, since the current evidence does not demonstrate training effectiveness or clinical validity.

2) Is the paper technically sound?: The paper is technically plausible and the system architecture is generally well motivated. The comparison with XPBD and VegaFEM, the volume-preservation analysis, and the runtime measurements provide useful supporting evidence. However, several technical aspects require clarification before the paper can be considered fully sound. The haptic channel is insufficiently characterized: the authors report a 1000 Hz haptic loop and serial communication, but they do not report end-to-end latency, force range, force calibration, saturation, filtering, interpolation between physics and haptic updates, or measured output force. The formative evaluation is also very limited, involving only one surgical expert, and the haptics-off/haptics-on comparison is confounded by different lesion positions and fixed task order. Therefore, the study supports feasibility, but not efficacy.

3) Is the subject matter presented in a comprehensive manner?: The paper is generally clear and covers the system architecture, physical simulation, interaction design, technical validation, and formative evaluation. The limitations are also discussed more openly than in many prototype papers. Nevertheless, some important details are missing. The lesion geometry, depth, size, and placement strategy should be described more precisely. The authors should explain how comparable the haptics-off and haptics-on conditions were, and whether lesion difficulty was controlled. More information is also needed about the haptic device specifications, the force mapping, and synchronization between the simulation frame rate and the haptic loop. The educational claims should be moderated unless supported by a structured user study with novices and experts, quantitative localization metrics, and learning outcomes.

4) Are the references provided applicable and sufficient?: The references are broadly applicable. They cover haptic feedback in medical simulation, soft-tissue simulation, FEM, XPBD/PBD, liver palpation, and the authors’ previous work. However, the bibliography is relatively short for an IEEE Access article and could be strengthened with additional references on validation methodology for medical simulators, palpation training systems, haptic-rendering evaluation, and user-study design in surgical simulation. The paper would also benefit from a clearer comparison with recent liver or abdominal palpation simulators, not only with general haptic or FEM methods.

5) Are there references that are not appropriate for the topic being discussed?: No

5a) If yes, then please indicate which references should be removed.: I did not identify references that should clearly be removed. The cited educational feedback and simulation-based learning papers are somewhat general, but they are relevant if the authors use them only to motivate the educational context. They should not be used to imply that this prototype has demonstrated educational effectiveness. But no specific reference needs to be removed.


Reviewer: 2

Comments:
The paper presents an interesting and well-implemented haptic simulation framework for liver lesion localization training. The integration of real-time GB-cFEM simulation with haptic feedback is technically valuable and clearly documented. However, the evaluation is limited to a single expert participant and does not provide evidence of educational effectiveness. The manuscript would be strengthened by expanding the discussion of clinical relevance, providing a deeper comparison with related systems, and further clarifying the limitations of the current haptic and anatomical models.

Required Changes Before Acceptance
Expand the discussion on clinical relevance and potential educational impact.
Better justify the use of a single-expert formative evaluation.
Clarify limitations regarding haptic realism and anatomical fidelity.
Include a more detailed comparison with existing liver palpation simulators and training systems.
